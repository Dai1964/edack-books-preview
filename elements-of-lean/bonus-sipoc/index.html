Elements of Lean · Bonus lesson

What is SIPOC? Teach it to your child with a lemonade stall

SIPOC is a one-page map of a process, in five columns: Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers. You draw it before you change anything, so everyone sees the whole picture first.

🍋 Play it first: The Lean Heroes at the Summer Fayre

A free game made for this lesson. Your child plans a lemonade stall with SIPOC, then runs it on fayre day. Ten minutes, on a phone or tablet.

Play the free game

1 The idea in one minute

Start with a real stall. The Lean Heroes want to sell lemonade at the village fayre. Before anyone squeezes a lemon, they lay out five columns.

A SIPOC for a lemonade stall
ColumnThe questionAt the stall
SuppliersWho gives us what we need?The corner shop, Grandma's lemon tree, the fayre committee
InputsWhat do we need?Lemons, sugar, ice, cups, a cash tin with change
ProcessWhat are the main steps?Squeeze and mix, pour and sell, tidy up
OutputsWhat comes out?Cups of lemonade, money for the dojo
CustomersWho gets it?The vicar, the footballers, Mrs Jones from the cake stall

That's all SIPOC is. It doesn't fix anything on its own. It shows you where the process starts, where it ends, and who is involved. Then you spot problems early: no cups packed, or nobody planning to tidy up.

Some things matter but don't go in a column. Sunny weather isn't a supplier: it changes how many customers come. Someone tasting the stock isn't a process step: it's waste.

2 Where it fits

SIPOC isn't in the first Lean Heroes book. It fits just before the first step of DMAIC.

In The Lean Heroes and the Dinner Disaster, Lauren the Owl starts the Define step by writing the problem in one sentence. A SIPOC is the picture around that sentence. Improvement teams often draw one at the start of a project, in Define, to agree the edges of the problem.

In the game, the stall's problems lead to DMAIC too. Your child picks one problem and sees how Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve and Control would fix it.

3 Teach it tonight (15 minutes)

You need: a sheet of paper, a pen, and tomorrow's breakfast.

  1. 2 minutes. Play the first stage of the game together, or just read the lemonade table above.
  2. 3 minutes. Draw five columns. Write S, I, P, O, C at the top. Say what each letter asks, using the table.
  3. 5 minutes. Fill it in for breakfast. Let your child do the writing. Who supplies the bread and milk? What goes in? What are the steps? What comes out? Who eats it?
  4. 3 minutes. Hunt for one thing that goes wrong at breakfast. Is something missing from Inputs, like no milk? Is a step missing from Process, like nobody clearing the table?
  5. 2 minutes. Pin the sheet on the fridge. Tomorrow morning, check it against what really happens.

If your child gets stuck, go back to the stall. Concrete first, names second.

4 The Feynman check

Can you explain the five letters to your child using your own kitchen? Then you've got it.

Try these three without looking:

  1. What does each letter of SIPOC stand for, and what question does it ask?
  2. Why is "sunny weather" not a supplier?
  3. Why do you draw a SIPOC before trying to fix anything?

Where you get stuck is the bit to read again. That's the useful part.

5 Next step

Next, pick one real problem from your breakfast SIPOC and write it in one sentence. That's the Define step.

One idea to keep from the book. It ends with the Lean Hero Pledge: I will never blame a person when I can fix a process. A SIPOC helps, because it looks at the whole process, not at who got it wrong.
