What is DMAIC? Teach it to your child with a dinner disaster

DMAIC is five steps for fixing a problem so it stays fixed: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control. You take them in that order, so you find the real cause before you try a fix.

It takes fifteen minutes together, and you both get a way to break down problems. Your child can use it at school, and you can use it at work.

The book ends with the Lean Hero Pledge: I will never blame a person when I can fix a process. (The Lean Heroes and the Dinner Disaster). DMAIC is how you keep that promise.

1 The idea in one minute

Start with a real kitchen. Dinner was meant to be ready at seven. It's late, and half of it is burnt. Here are the five steps, with that dinner as the example.

DMAIC for a dinner disaster
StepThe questionIn the kitchen
DefineWhat exactly is the problem?Write it in one sentence: "Dinner was 45 minutes late."
MeasureWhat really happened?Count the burnt dishes. Note when each job started and finished.
AnalyseWhy did it happen?Ask "Why?" again and again until you reach the root cause.
ImproveWhat change fixes the cause?Try one change, like a shared cooking plan, and test it.
ControlHow do we make it stick?Write the new steps down and check them next Friday.

The order is the point. Most of us jump straight from the problem to a fix. DMAIC makes you count first and ask why second, so you fix the cause and not just the symptom.

It isn't only for children. The American Society for Quality calls DMAIC a structured problem-solving approach for improving processes, used in Lean and Six Sigma.

2 Where it is in the book

DMAIC is the whole of The Lean Heroes and the Dinner Disaster. Every Friday, five woodland friends cook dinner together at 37 Edack Drive. This Friday, everything goes wrong: burnt soup, two salads, a smoking oven and a mystery stew.

Up in the Treehouse Dojo, the five letters are painted on the wall. Each friend leads one step:

  • Define: Lauren the Owl writes the problem in one sentence.
  • Measure: Ethan the Squirrel counts what actually happened, with a tally chart and a simple flow map.
  • Analyse: Aaron the Monkey climbs the Why Ladder to the root cause: no shared cooking plan.
  • Improve: Neo the Badger tests one change at a time.
  • Control: Master Taff shows them how to make the fix stick.

After each step there's a "Your Turn" page, so your child uses the tool on a problem of their own. The game has its own kitchen problems, so some answers differ from the book. The five steps are the same.

3 Teach it tonight (15 minutes)

You need: a sheet of paper, a pen, a clock, and tonight's bedtime.

  1. 2 minutes, before bedtime. Define. Write one sentence together, like "Lights out was 20 minutes late." Leave names out of it. It's about the routine, not a person.
  2. During bedtime. Measure. Your child writes down the clock time at each step: bath, teeth, pyjamas, story, lights out. Count before you guess.
  3. 5 minutes, after. Analyse. Find the longest gap. Ask "Why?" and keep asking until the answer is about the routine. "Nobody knew where the pyjamas were" is a cause you can fix.
  4. 3 minutes. Improve. Pick one change to test tomorrow, like laying out pyjamas before bath time. Just one, so you know what worked.
  5. 5 minutes, the next night. Control. Did it help? If it did, write the new routine on a card and stick it on the bedroom door.

If your child gets stuck, go back to the game. Concrete first, names second.

4 The Feynman check

Can you explain the five steps to your child using something from your own home, like a messy bedroom or a late school run? Then you've got it.

Try these three without looking:

  1. What are the five steps, in order, and what question does each one ask?
  2. Why do you measure before you decide what went wrong?
  3. Why should the last "Why?" point at the process, not at a person?

Where you get stuck is the bit to read again. That's the useful part.
